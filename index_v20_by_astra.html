01

A life

From a rice-trading family in Suphan Buri to the abbotship of Wat Paknam.

He was born Sod Mikaewnoi on 10 October B.E. 2427 (1884) at Song Phi Nong in Suphan Buri, the son of Ngoen and Sudjai, a family of rice traders. He learned to read from an uncle who was a monk, and worked the family trade along the canals before deciding to ordain.

He was ordained in July B.E. 2449 (1906), in his 22nd year, at Wat Song Phi Nong, and received the Pali name Candasaro. He then spent years moving between teachers in Bangkok, studying Pali scripture by day and meditation by night.

In the middle of his twelfth rains retreat, on the full-moon night of the tenth lunar month in B.E. 2460 (1917), he sat down at Wat Bot Bon in Bang Khu Wiang, Nonthaburi, having resolved not to rise until he saw what the Buddha saw. What he described afterwards — an inner body of Dhamma reached through the centre of one's own body — became the meditation system known as Vijja Dhammakaya.

As abbot of Wat Paknam Bhasicharoen, he restored the temple and supported Buddhist study and meditation. He established a monastic school, attracting monks, novices, mae chi and lay practitioners.

He died at Wat Paknam on 3 February B.E. 2502 (1959), aged 74, in his 53rd year as a monk.

02

What he taught

A reading guide to the excerpt, with background on Vijja Dhammakaya. These explanations are editorial summaries, not verbatim quotations.

CORE

The body of Dhamma within

Every person already carries the Dhammakaya inside them. Meditation is not the manufacture of something new but the uncovering of what is there.

Read more

Luang Por Sod taught that a succession of ever-subtler bodies lies at the centre of the human body — human, celestial, Brahma, formless Brahma, and finally the Dhammakaya. Attention resting steadily at the seventh base, the centre of the body, is what opens that sequence.

PRACTICE

Go to the end of yourself

The instruction repeated most often in the recording: keep going inward until there is no further inward to reach.

Read more

In the excerpt he urges listeners to reach the end of themselves, describing this as the point where they can look after themselves and are no longer commanded by others.

FREEDOM

Who is giving the orders

Whoever has not reached that limit is still being moved — to anger, to violence, to want — by something other than themselves.

Read more

The excerpt describes Mara as directing people to curse, strike and kill. This summary preserves the imagery of the talk without deciding how that imagery should be interpreted.

THE POINT

The bullseye

He calls this the heart of the matter and warns against drifting — the Thai word he repeats, ไหลเล่อ, is closer to "wandering off vaguely".

Read more

The talk uses examples from household life to urge listeners to examine themselves and not lose sight of practice. The excerpt does not establish the identity of its audience.

FOUNDATION

Hearing, then training

Ignorance persists, he says, because people have neither heard the Dhamma of the Buddha and the arahants nor trained the mind in it.

Read more

The closing passage names both hearing the Dhamma and training the mind in it.

RESULT

Your own refuge

Reach the point where no further body lies beyond, and a person is finally able to be their own support.

Read more

In this excerpt, being able to look after oneself is connected with reaching the end of one’s own body. This card summarizes that passage without equating it with a separate scriptural quotation.
